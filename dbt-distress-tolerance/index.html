DBT distress tolerance: the pause before you react

A pause is a distress-tolerance skill—a way to get through an intense moment without making it worse. This guide places Foothold Now’s Pause practice in its tradition, Dialectical Behavior Therapy, and reviews the research behind it. It is written for the curious reader; it is not clinical advice.

What distress tolerance is

Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT) was developed by Marsha Linehan as a treatment that pairs change-oriented strategies with acceptance (Linehan, 1993). One of its four skill modules is distress tolerance: a set of skills for surviving a crisis moment without resorting to behavior that brings relief now but harm later (Linehan, 2015). The aim is not to fix the situation or make the feeling disappear, but to ride out the peak of an emotion so that a person can respond from a wiser place once the wave has crested.

The STOP skill

Among the crisis-survival skills, STOP is the one most directly about the moment before a reaction (Linehan, 2015). The acronym stands for Stop, Take a step back, Observe, and Proceed mindfully. Foothold Now’s Pause practice follows the same arc:

Stop
Do not act on the urge yet—hold off on the message, the apology, the withdrawal, the confrontation.
Take a step back & observe
Name what is present—the feelings and the urges—because naming loosens their grip.
Rate and slow down
Read the intensity, then let the body settle with slow breathing, so the peak has time to fall.
Proceed mindfully
Choose a next step on purpose rather than being swept into one.

Checking the facts

A companion DBT skill, checking the facts, rests on a simple observation: emotions are often driven less by an event itself than by our interpretation of it (Linehan, 2015). The intensity of a feeling is real, but the story attached to it—what someone meant, what happens next—may not be confirmed. The closing line of the Pause practice (“my feelings are real, but my interpretation hasn’t been confirmed yet”) is a plain-language version of this skill: it creates a small gap between a feeling and the conclusion it seems to demand.

The evidence

DBT as a whole has a substantial evidence base, particularly for reducing self-harm and stabilizing emotion regulation. A meta-analysis using mixed-effects modeling found DBT produced a moderate global effect across controlled trials for borderline personality disorder (Kliem et al., 2010). It is important to be precise about scope: this evidence concerns the full, multi-component treatment delivered by trained clinicians, not any single skill practiced alone. A brief self-guided pause is best understood as drawn from an evidence-based treatment, not as a validated stand-alone intervention.

How this fits with exposure and the ladders

Most of Foothold Now is built on exposure and response prevention (ERP)—approaching what you avoid, on purpose, so that fear can settle through new learning (Foa & Kozak, 1986; Craske et al., 2014). That is planned practice you schedule for yourself. A pause is different in kind: a distress-tolerance skill for an unplanned hard moment. The two are complementary. Distress tolerance can make exposure more doable, by helping a person stay with discomfort rather than escape it, and acceptance-based skills and exposure appear to share overlapping mechanisms even as they differ in emphasis (Arch & Craske, 2008). In short, the ladders build tolerance for fear on your own schedule; the pause steadies you when a moment lands out of nowhere. Neither replaces the other, and the pause is offered here as a complement to the exposure mission of the site, not a substitute for it.

A clinical caution

Foothold Now is a personal practice companion, not treatment, and nothing here is medical advice. DBT is most effective as a structured program with a trained therapist. If you are in crisis or thinking of harming yourself, please contact a crisis line or someone you trust right now; in the US you can call or text 988.

Common questions

What is distress tolerance in DBT?

Distress tolerance is a set of DBT skills for getting through a crisis moment without making it worse: surviving the peak of an intense emotion rather than acting on an urge that brings relief now but harm later. It doesn’t try to fix the situation or remove the feeling; it helps you ride out the wave until you can respond more wisely.

What does the DBT STOP skill stand for?

STOP stands for Stop, Take a step back, Observe, and Proceed mindfully. You pause instead of reacting, step back from the urge, notice what is actually happening inside and around you, then choose a next action on purpose.

Can I practice a DBT pause on my own?

A brief pause can be practiced on your own, and Foothold Now offers a guided version. But the strongest evidence is for full DBT delivered by a trained therapist, so a self-guided pause is best seen as a skill drawn from an evidence-based treatment, not a replacement for it.

How is a DBT pause different from ERP?

ERP is planned practice: you approach what you avoid on purpose so fear can settle over time. A DBT pause is for an unplanned hard moment, to help you tolerate distress without reacting. They’re complementary, and staying steady in a hard moment can make exposure practice more doable.
