Using notes to map an OCD loop

OCD often runs on a loop: an intrusive thought arrives, distress rises, a compulsion brings quick relief, and over time the brain learns to lean on the ritual. Taking that loop apart on paper can make a hard step easier to face. This is an optional, deeper exploration, though—you don’t need to do it for every ladder or every step; reach for it when you want to work a tricky one through with these strategies. In Foothold Now, the Notes field on each ladder step—with its optional guide questions—is a natural place for it.

The loop, in one picture

1. Obsessionan intrusive thought, image, or urge shows up, uninvited
↓
2. Distressa feared outcome comes to mind—“if I don’t act, something bad might happen”
↓
3. Compulsion or avoidancea ritual (physical or mental), or steering around the trigger entirely
↓
4. Relief, then reinforcementthe worry eases for a moment, so the pull to repeat the ritual grows a little each time

The way out of this loop is exposure and response prevention (ERP): approach the trigger on purpose and skip the ritual, so your brain gets to find out what actually happens. But that only works if you know, in advance, exactly what the ritual is, and that is what unpacking gives you.

Unpack the loop in writing

Before you rate anything, name the pieces. You don’t have to solve them—just get them out of your head and onto the page.

  • Obsessions, intrusive thoughts, and urges. The trigger thought, image, or urge, in plain words. You don’t have to believe it, agree with it, or make it go away—just write what it says.
  • The feared outcome behind the ritual. The “if I don’t do this, then ___” at the heart of it. This is the engine of the loop, and often the hardest thing to name. Naming it is what makes the rest specific.
  • Physical compulsions. The visible actions: washing, checking, redoing, arranging, re-reading, seeking reassurance.
  • Mental compulsions. The invisible ones—reviewing, counting, silently reassuring yourself, replaying a memory, neutralizing a thought with another thought. These are easy to miss, and they matter most: a hidden mental ritual can quietly cancel out an exposure without you noticing.
  • Avoided situations. The places, objects, people, or activities you steer around so the obsession never has to fire in the first place. Avoidance is a compulsion in disguise, and every avoided situation is a future ladder step.
  • The responses to prevent. For each step, decide up front which compulsion or avoidance you will hold back during the exposure. Deciding this before you start—rather than in the heat of the moment—is what makes response prevention possible.

An optional note template

When a step is worth the extra thought, you can give it its own plan. Turn on Guide questions in the step’s Notes field and these prompts appear for you to fill in whichever lines help—it keeps your rule clear before you start and gives you something to reflect on afterward. Check the box to see the questions or uncheck it for a plain note, whichever you like. There is no need to do this for every step:

Exposure
What will I approach?
Prediction
What am I afraid could happen?
Usual response
What compulsion or avoidance do I normally use?
Response-prevention commitment
What will I refrain from doing?
Learning afterward
What did I learn about tolerating uncertainty?

The rating holds the difficulty; the note holds the rules. Filling in Learning afterward once the discomfort has settled is where the loop actually loosens—you’re collecting evidence that you can carry uncertainty without the ritual.

A worked example

Say the worry is “what if I left the front door unlocked?” The feared outcome is that something will go wrong and it will be your fault for not checking. The physical compulsion is going back to check the lock, sometimes more than once; the mental ones are picturing the door in your mind and reassuring yourself that you “definitely locked it.” An avoided situation might be being the last one to leave the house. Mapped onto a ladder, with a rule on each step:

Lock the door once, then walk away without checking30
Leave the house and don’t go back to check for ten minutes45
Be the last to leave, lock up once, and head out60
Leave for the whole day after a single lock, no returning75

A filled-in note on that first step might read: Exposure — lock the door once and walk away. Prediction — I’ll feel unsure and worry I left it open. Usual response — go back and check two or three times, picture the lock, tell myself it’s fine. Response prevention — check once only, no going back, no picturing it. Afterward — the doubt rose and eased on its own; I can leave with a little uncertainty and be okay.

How unpacking helps

  • It turns a vague fog of dread into concrete steps you can actually rate and climb.
  • It makes response prevention explicit and repeatable—you decide the rule once, in writing, instead of negotiating with yourself mid-exposure.
  • It surfaces the hidden mental rituals that would otherwise quietly undo your progress.
  • It gives you a record to reflect on: did I keep my rule, and what did I learn? is easy to check when it’s written down.

A gentle caution

ERP is the best-supported approach for OCD, but it is genuinely easier and kinder with a therapist trained in it, precisely because mental rituals and reassurance-seeking are so easy to miss on your own. Foothold Now is a practice companion, not treatment, and nothing here is medical advice. If you’re working with a clinician, map the loop and build your ladders together.

Common questions

What’s the difference between a physical and a mental compulsion?

Physical compulsions are visible actions—washing, checking, redoing, seeking reassurance. Mental compulsions are internal rituals—reviewing, counting, silently reassuring yourself, neutralizing a thought. Mental ones are easy to miss, and naming them matters because they can quietly cancel out an exposure.

Why write down the feared outcome?

The feared outcome is the engine of the loop—the “if I don’t do this, something bad happens” belief. Naming it turns a vague dread into something specific you can approach on purpose, and it shows you exactly which response to hold back.

What does response prevention mean on a step?

It’s the rule you keep during the exposure: the compulsion or avoidance you agree not to do while you sit with the discomfort. Writing it in the step’s Notes field makes it clear before you start.
