How to stop avoiding things

The unopened mail. The call you keep not making. The highway exit you quietly route around. Avoidance buys relief now—it charges interest later. Here’s a practical way out.

The loop that keeps you stuck

Avoiding something hard works, briefly. The dread drops the moment you decide not today, and that relief is a reward—so the habit strengthens. Meanwhile the thing itself usually grows: the email gets more awkward to answer, the skill gets rustier, the story in your head gets scarier than the reality ever was. Relief now, interest later.

The way out isn’t willpower or a burst of courage. It’s the opposite of dramatic: approach the thing on purpose, in small doses, repeatedly—at a size where you can stay with the discomfort and find out what actually happens.

Make it a ladder

Take the thing you’re avoiding and break it into steps, then rate each one 0–100 by how hard it feels right now (a SUDS rating). That’s a fear ladder—what practitioners call an exposure hierarchy. Someone avoiding phone calls might build:

Listen to the voicemail all the way through25
Write down the three things I need to say30
Call when I know it’ll go to voicemail, leave a message45
Make the call to the office during opening hours60
Make the harder call I’ve put off for months80

Start around 30–40—uncomfortable, but doable. Stay with the step without your usual escape hatches: no handing the phone to someone else, no “I’ll just email instead,” no rehearsing it forty times first. Notice how it feels before, at the peak, and after. Then do it again another day. Steps settle with repetition, and each settled step makes the one above it smaller.

What counts as avoidance

More than you’d think. Procrastination is often avoidance of how a task feels to start. So is over-preparing, keeping conversations shallow, always taking the same route, checking everything twice, or staying endlessly “busy.” The tell is the relief: if skipping it relaxes you and shrinks your life a little, it belongs on a ladder.

When it’s more than a habit

Sometimes avoidance is part of something with a name—social anxiety, panic, or the rituals of OCD. The approach stays the same at heart, and the evidence behind it is strongest exactly there: gradual exposure for anxiety, and exposure and response prevention (ERP) for OCD. A therapist trained in these can make the climb faster and kinder; if you’re working with one, build your ladders together.

Common questions

Why does avoidance make anxiety worse?

Skipping the hard thing brings instant relief, and that relief rewards the avoidance—so the habit strengthens while the fear stays untested and grows.

Is procrastination a form of avoidance?

Often, yes. Procrastination is frequently avoidance of how a task feels to start, not the task itself—which is why it responds to the same small-steps approach.
